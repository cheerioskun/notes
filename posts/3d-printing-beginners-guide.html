3Dfield notes for making physical things
Beginner's guide

3D printing is not magic. It is slow sculpture with a compiler.

A printer turns a digital model into a stack of warm plastic decisions. The good prints come less from owning a machine and more from learning where those decisions hide: orientation, tolerances, material, support, and the first layer.

Most hobby printers use FDM: a controlled nozzle melts filament and lays it down one path at a time. Think CNC hot glue, not Star Trek replicator.

01 / mechanism

What you are actually doing

For a first useful, good-looking print, treat 3D printing as a four-part system: geometry, slicing, material, and machine condition. A bad print is rarely just “the printer being weird.” It is usually one of those four parts leaking into the others.

The printer does not understand your object. It understands toolpaths.

The slicer is the compiler. It takes a mesh or CAD body and emits thousands of movements: where the nozzle goes, how hot it is, how fast it moves, how much plastic it pushes, and when the fan turns on. If the model has tiny details, unsupported ledges, thin walls, or impossible tolerances, the slicer will still produce instructions. They just may not produce the object in your head.

Model

The shape you want. Usually STL, 3MF, or an exported mesh from CAD.

Slicer

The translation layer. PrusaSlicer, OrcaSlicer, Cura, or Bambu Studio turn shape into G-code.

Print

The physical negotiation: heat, cooling, grip, gravity, shrinkage, and layer adhesion.

Trap
Do not start by printing something huge. The beginner mistake is spending eight hours discovering a mistake that a twenty-minute test cube, tolerance gauge, or small fragment would have exposed.
02 / workflow

The practical workflow

Almost every successful print follows the same loop. It is not glamorous, but it is the loop that makes the hobby predictable.

01

Choose the job

Decorative object, functional bracket, container, miniature, lithophane, mechanism. The job determines material and design.

02

Model or remix

Use CAD for precise objects, Blender for sculptural forms, or remix an existing model with permission.

03

Slice

Pick orientation, supports, layer height, walls, infill, brim, temperature, and speed.

04

Print a probe

Print the smallest piece that tests the risky bit: fit, text, overhang, surface, hinge, or wall thickness.

05

Finish

Remove supports, sand, deburr, glue, paint, add hardware, or reprint with the lesson learned.

If you are making a thoughtful personal object, do not confuse complexity with care. A clean shape, good material choice, tuned print orientation, and a little finishing will beat an overcomplicated model with stringing and scars.

04 / mental models

Mental models that save prints

Orientation is design

The same model printed upright, sideways, or on its back can have different strength, surface quality, support scars, and print time. Orientation is not an afterthought.

Layer lines are grain

FDM parts are weaker between layers, like wood splitting along grain. Put force across layers when possible, not peeling them apart.

Tolerance is a budget

If two parts must fit, design clearance. A snug FDM slip fit often needs extra room. Test your own printer instead of trusting a universal number.

Support is debt

Supports let you print impossible overhangs, then charge interest as rough surfaces, waste, removal time, and occasional broken details.

The first layer is a contract

If the first layer does not stick, everything above it inherits the lie. Clean bed, correct Z offset, and sane speed matter.

Plastic remembers heat

Warping is uneven cooling. Stringing is uncontrolled oozing. Brittle parts can be bad layer bonding, wet filament, or wrong temperature.

05 / design

Design rules for a first object

A first designed print should be emotionally specific but mechanically simple. The best candidates are shallow reliefs, small desk objects, custom containers, key trays, display stands, ornaments, bookmarks, nameplates, lamp shades, or objects that hold a tiny physical memory: a date, a place outline, a soundwave, a coordinate, a favorite phrase set as geometry.

RuleBeginner-safe versionWhy
Keep the base flatGive the print a stable face that sits on the bed.Flat bases reduce supports, warping, and first-layer drama.
Use thick featuresAvoid hairline text, needles, tiny walls, and fragile stems.A common 0.4 mm nozzle cannot render every detail you can draw.
Prefer chamfersUse 45-degree chamfers instead of unsupported horizontal ledges.Printers can handle slopes more easily than ceilings.
Prototype the risky areaPrint one corner, one hinge, one letter, or one fitted hole first.Fast local tests save full-object failures.
Hide the seamPut the slicer's seam on a back edge or less visible face.The nozzle starts and stops somewhere. Decide where the scar lives.
Trap
Text is harder than it looks. Engraved text collects shadows and can fill in. Raised text catches light but can be fragile. Use bolder fonts, larger sizes, and print a small text strip before committing.
06 / materials

Materials, settings, and failure modes

Start with PLA or PLA+. It is forgiving, crisp, available in beautiful colors, and good for decorative or light functional objects. PETG is tougher and more heat-resistant but stringier. TPU is flexible. ABS and ASA are useful but bring fumes, warping, and enclosure requirements.

MaterialUse whenBeginner note
PLA / PLA+Decor, prototypes, toys, display pieces, containers away from heatBest first material. Not for hot cars or high heat.
PETGTougher brackets, outdoor-ish use, parts needing more flexCan string and blob. Dry filament helps.
TPUFlexible feet, grips, bumpers, casesPrint slower. Direct-drive extruders handle it better.
ABS / ASAHeat resistance, automotive-ish parts, outdoor partsNeeds ventilation and usually an enclosure. Not a first-week material.

Settings worth understanding early

  • Layer height: lower is smoother and slower. Higher is faster and more visibly layered.
  • Walls/perimeters: more walls often make parts stronger than simply adding infill.
  • Infill: internal lattice. Many decorative parts do not need much.
  • Temperature: affects flow, gloss, stringing, and layer bonding.
  • Cooling: helps overhangs and details, but too much can weaken bonding for some materials.
  • Supports: use as little as the geometry permits. Tree/organic supports can reduce scarring on some shapes.

Pre-print checklist

Run this before the “real” print, especially if the object has personal stakes.

Things to take with you

  • The slicer is the compiler; learn to read its preview.
  • Orientation changes strength, scars, time, and beauty.
  • Supports are useful, but every support is a future cleanup job.
  • Calipers and test pieces beat hope.
  • Finish the object. The last ten percent is where it stops looking like a printer demo.
